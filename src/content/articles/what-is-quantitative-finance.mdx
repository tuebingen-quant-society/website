Quantitative finance is not one job, and it is not a collection of secret
formulas. It is a way of working: turn a question about markets into something
that can be investigated with data, mathematics, and code. The result may be a
forecast, but it does not have to be. Quants also calculate prices, measure
risk, and build systems that execute decisions reliably.

In one sentence:

> Quantitative finance combines financial markets with statistics,
> mathematics, computer science, and scientific testing to make decisions
> under uncertainty.

## Four examples that look very different

### 1. Modelling a price and its risk

What is an option worth? What happens to a portfolio if interest rates,
volatility, or exchange rates jump? Quants build models and simulations to
answer questions like these. A model is not a perfect copy of reality. It is a
deliberate simplification whose assumptions and errors need to be understood.

### 2. Making markets and executing orders

A market maker continuously quotes prices at which it is willing to buy and
sell. It has to combine the spread, its current inventory, new information, and
the risk of a sudden price move. Other teams study how to execute a large order
without moving the market against themselves unnecessarily. Probability,
optimisation, and highly reliable software meet directly in this work.

### 3. Searching data for signals

A research team might test whether certain information explains future
returns, volatility, or trading volume. The inputs can range from prices and
company accounts to text, supply chains, and satellite images. Machine learning
can help find patterns in large or unstructured datasets, but it cannot replace
a good question or a clean experiment.

### 4. Estimating oil inventories from shadows

The well-known satellite example is real, although it is more precise than
simply “looking at the shadows of oil depots.” Many crude-oil tanks have a
floating roof that rests directly on the liquid. When the fill level falls, the
roof falls with it. The tank wall then casts a different shadow across the
lowered roof. The roof height—and therefore the fill level—can be estimated
from that geometry, the tank dimensions, and the sun angle. [Wang et al.
describe one method using high-resolution satellite
imagery](https://www.mdpi.com/2072-4292/11/7/793).

This can produce an estimate before aggregated storage figures are published,
and computer vision can process many sites at scale. It is not a magical view
of the market, however. The method does not work equally well for every tank
type, clouds and image quality limit observations, and a better inventory
estimate is not automatically a reliable price forecast. An [empirical study
of cloud cover and oil markets](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41599-023-02128-5)
is a useful example of how the measurement method, data availability, and the
market question interact.

## Where machine learning fits

Machine learning is a toolkit within quantitative finance, not its definition.
A linear model may be a better fit for one problem than a neural network. In
other cases, gradient boosting, deep learning, or language models help because
the volume or form of the data is more complex.

Financial data make ML unusually difficult:

- Markets change, so an old pattern may not remain stable.
- A tiny data leak can make a backtest look convincing and be useless.
- Fees, spreads, and market impact can consume a statistical edge.
- Correlation is not automatically a durable economic explanation.

Baselines, out-of-sample tests, and stress scenarios therefore matter just as
much as training the model. [Jane Street's description of quantitative
research](https://www.janestreet.com/join-jane-street/position/8573726002/)
similarly combines experiment design, dataset generation, time-series
analysis, feature engineering, and different statistical and ML methods.

## A typical quant workflow

1. **Define the question.** What should be estimated, priced, optimised, or hedged?
2. **Understand the data.** Where did they come from, when would they actually have been available, and what biases do they contain?
3. **Build a simple baseline.** Show that a more complex approach beats a clear reference.
4. **Test the model.** Use later test data, realistic costs, and alternative assumptions.
5. **Check robustness.** Does the result hold in other periods and market regimes? Where does it break?
6. **Make and operate the decision.** Set position sizes, limits, monitoring, and the technical implementation.

A beautiful backtest is only an intermediate result. What matters is whether
the whole chain from information to controlled decision holds together.

## What roles are there?

Titles differ between firms and the boundaries often overlap, but four broad
areas appear repeatedly:

- **Quant researcher:** develops hypotheses, analyses data, and builds and validates models or strategies.
- **Quant trader:** makes and monitors trading decisions, understands market mechanics, and gives research direct feedback from live trading.
- **Quant developer or research engineer:** builds data pipelines, simulations, and production systems so research is reproducible and can run under real conditions.
- **Quant analyst, portfolio quant, or risk quant:** measures exposures, constructs portfolios, develops scenarios, and supports investment or risk decisions.

In practice these roles work closely together. [IMC describes quant research
as collaboration among researchers, traders, and
engineers](https://www.imc.com/us/careers/experienced-roles/quant-research),
while [Citadel places software engineering](https://www.citadel.com/careers/details/software-engineer/)
across research, analysis, trading, risk management, and settlement. “Quant” is
better understood as a spectrum than as one job description.

## How can you get started?

You do not need to begin with a finance degree. A practical foundation has four
parts:

- probability, statistics, and some linear algebra
- Python and careful work with data
- the basics of markets, financial products, and risk
- small projects whose assumptions and failure modes remain visible

A first project could be a backtest **with** transaction costs and a genuinely
later test period. A Monte Carlo option simulation, a small order-book model,
or an investigation of why an attractive signal disappears after realistic
costs are equally useful.

Quantitative finance does not mean predicting markets with certainty. It means
making uncertainty measurable, testing ideas hard, and building systems that
work outside a notebook. That combination of theory, data, and practical
implementation is what makes the field so broad.
