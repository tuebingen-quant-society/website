## Futures

A derivative is a contract whose value depends on another price. The simplest
one is the future: fix the price today, buy or sell later.

<Terms>

- **Long and short** The buyer is long, the seller is short. Payment happens at expiry.
- **Margin** Instead of the full price you post collateral, often 5 to 20%. That is where leverage comes from.
- **Mark-to-market** Gains and losses are settled against your account as they happen.
- **Basis** The gap between future and spot. Annualised, it is your return in a cash and carry.

</Terms>

<BasisLab lang="en" />

<Takeaways title="In short">

- The fair price is spot plus financing cost: $$F = S \cdot e^{rT}$$.
- If the future trades above that, buy spot and sell the future. The profit is
  fixed from day one, wherever the price ends up.
- It is not risk free: if the price rallies hard, the short side needs more
  margin even though your spot gains just as much.

</Takeaways>

<Details summary="Why F = S · e^(rT)?">

You need bitcoin in a year. Route one: buy it today for 100 and hold it; that
ties up capital that would otherwise earn 4% interest. Route two: buy a future.
Both routes have to cost the same, or there is an arbitrage. So the future sits
at about 104.

<Formula>

$$
F = S \cdot e^{rT}
$$

- $$S$$: spot price
- $$r$$: interest rate p.a. (financing cost)
- $$T$$: time to expiry in years

</Formula>

This is not a model of the future but an arbitrage argument.

</Details>

<Details summary="Contango, backwardation and settlement">

A future above spot is in **contango**; below spot, in **backwardation**. In
crypto, contango is the norm: many traders want leveraged long exposure and pay
a premium for it.

At expiry the underlying is either delivered (physical) or only the difference
is paid (cash settlement). Crypto futures on CME and Deribit settle in cash.

</Details>

## Options

An option is a right, not an obligation: you only buy or sell if it pays. That
right costs a premium.

<Terms>

- **Call** The right to buy. Pays when the price ends above the strike.
- **Put** The right to sell. Pays when the price ends below the strike.
- **Strike** The price at which you may buy or sell.
- **Premium** What the right costs. The buyer can lose at most this.

</Terms>

<PayoffLab lang="en" />

<Takeaways title="In short">

- Solid line: profit or loss at expiry. Dashed: the value today. The gap is the
  **time value**; it melts away until expiry.
- Option sellers collect the premium for sure and carry the risk of large moves
  in return.
- Combinations of calls, puts and the underlying build almost any profile.

</Takeaways>

<Details summary="European, American and moneyness">

European options can only be exercised at expiry, American ones at any time.
Most crypto options, for example on Deribit, are European.

A call with strike 90 at a price of 100 is _in the money_, with strike 110 _out
of the money_, with strike 100 _at the money_.

</Details>

</Module>

## Pricing

In 1973 Fischer Black and Myron Scholes showed how to rebuild an option from
the underlying and cash. What the rebuild costs is the fair price.

<OptionPricer lang="en" />

<Takeaways title="In short">

- Every input is observable except volatility. It is backed out of the market
  price: the **implied volatility**.
- The **Greeks** measure how sensitive the price is. Delta is how much of the
  underlying you effectively hold; theta is what each day costs you.
- Put-call parity holds without any model: a call minus a put is a future.

</Takeaways>

<Details summary="The formula">

<Formula>

$$
\begin{aligned}
C &= S \, N(d_1) - K e^{-rT} N(d_2) \\[0.5em]
d_1 &= \frac{\ln(S/K) + \left(r + \frac{\sigma^2}{2}\right) T}{\sigma \sqrt{T}} \\[0.5em]
d_2 &= d_1 - \sigma \sqrt{T}
\end{aligned}
$$

- $$N$$: cumulative distribution function of the standard normal
- $$\sigma$$: volatility p.a.

</Formula>

That is why option traders often talk about vol rather than prices: "the
30-day call trades at 55 vol."

</Details>

<Details summary="The Greeks at a glance">

| Greek | Measures                                 | Rule of thumb                        |
| ----- | ---------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------ |
| Delta | Price change per 1 in the underlying     | ATM call ≈ 0.5                       |
| Gamma | Change in delta per 1 in the underlying  | largest ATM and close to expiry      |
| Vega  | Price change per vol point               | longer expiries react more           |
| Theta | Price change per day                     | buyers lose time value every day     |

</Details>

Not every option has a formula. Then you simulate: roll many price paths,
average the payoffs, discount.

## Perpetuals

A perp is a future that never expires, and today the most traded product in
crypto. With no expiry to pull it to the spot price, another mechanism takes
over: funding.

<Terms>

- **Funding** Longs and shorts pay each other a small amount every few hours. The direction pulls the perp towards spot.
- **Premium** How far the perp trades above or below spot.
- **Leverage** At leverage L you post only 1/L of the notional.
- **Liquidation** If the price moves roughly 1/L against you, the exchange closes your position.

</Terms>

<FundingLab lang="en" />

High leverage is normal on perps, 50× and more. The stress test shows what that
means.

<LiquidationLab lang="en" />

<Takeaways title="In short">

- Positive funding: longs pay shorts. In the flat zone it sits at 0.01% per 8
  hours, which is 10.95% a year.
- At 20× leverage liquidation is just under 5% away; at 60% volatility that is
  less than two normal days.

</Takeaways>

<Details summary="The funding formula">

<Formula>

$$
\text{Funding} = P + \operatorname{clamp}_{\pm 0.05\,\%}\left(I - P\right)
$$

- $$P$$: premium index, the averaged gap between perp and spot
- $$I$$: interest component, usually 0.01% per 8 hours
- $$\operatorname{clamp}_{\pm a}$$: limited to the range −a to +a

</Formula>

Binance and BitMEX pay BTC funding every 8 hours, Hyperliquid every hour (one
eighth of the 8-hour rate each time). According to the
[BitMEX derivatives report for Q3 2025](https://blog.bitmex.com/2025q3-derivatives-report)
BTC funding was positive more than 92% of the time.

</Details>

<Details summary="Liquidation price and auto-deleveraging">

<Formula>

$$
\begin{aligned}
\text{Long:} \quad P_{\text{liq}} &\approx P_{\text{entry}} \left(1 - \tfrac{1}{L} + m\right) \\[0.5em]
\text{Short:} \quad P_{\text{liq}} &\approx P_{\text{entry}} \left(1 + \tfrac{1}{L} - m\right)
\end{aligned}
$$

- $$L$$: leverage
- $$m$$: maintenance margin, e.g. 0.5%

</Formula>

If the margin does not cover a liquidation, the exchange's insurance fund steps
in. If that is empty, profitable positions on the other side are cut
automatically (**auto-deleveraging**). It hits exactly the traders who are
winning.

</Details>

<Details summary="Where perps come from">

The idea goes back to a 1993 paper by Robert Shiller. It took off in 2016 when
BitMEX launched the first bitcoin perp. Today perps trade more volume than the
spot market.

</Details>

## Crypto markets

The maths is the same as in traditional markets. What differs is the market
structure, and that is where the opportunities come from.

<MarketMap lang="en" />

Many decentralised exchanges have no order book but a pool with one rule: the
product of the reserves stays constant, $$x \cdot y = k$$. The price follows
from their ratio.

<AmmLab lang="en" />

<Takeaways title="In short">

- The larger your order relative to the pool, the worse your average price.
- The pool does not know the market price. Arbitrageurs keep it right and get
  paid for it.

</Takeaways>

<Details summary="More on AMMs">

As the [Uniswap v2 whitepaper](https://uniswap.org/whitepaper.pdf) describes,
the gap after each trade is by design: traders' fees pay liquidity providers for
the risk that arbitrageurs profit at their expense. The gap is often closed
within the same block.

</Details>

</Module>

## Arbitrage

In an arbitrage the profit is almost fixed at entry. It is rarely free: you pay
with capital, speed or a risk that does not appear in the model.

The classic in crypto is the funding carry: long spot, short perp, same
notional. Price moves cancel out and the funding is what remains.

<FundingCarryLab lang="en" />

Faster and smaller are price gaps between exchanges, or between three pairs on
one exchange.

<ArbitrageLab lang="en" />

<Takeaways title="In short">

- The funding carry pays as you go and can be closed at any time; a cash and
  carry with futures locks the return in until expiry.
- Fees and slippage decide. Calculate with the price you actually get filled
  at.

</Takeaways>

<Details summary="How cross-exchange arbitrage works in practice">

Nobody moves coins back and forth for it; that takes too long. You hold
inventory on both exchanges and rebalance now and then. Those who collect these
gaps reliably have fast connections, low fee tiers and code that decides within
milliseconds.

</Details>

## Strategies

Nine strategies on one map: on the right the profit is fixed at entry, on the
left there is only a statistical edge. At the top speed matters, at the bottom
patience and capital.

<StrategyExplorer lang="en" />

<Takeaways title="What goes wrong in practice">

- **Counterparty**: an exchange halts withdrawals. Both legs are gone.
- **Hedge liquidated**: in a fast rally the short side goes, and you are left
  only long.
- **Funding flips**: after a crash it is often negative for days.
- **Stablecoins**: your "dollar" is a token with its own risk.
- **Overfitting**: a backtest with many parameters almost always finds
  something.

</Takeaways>
