## What a derivative is

A derivative is a contract whose value depends on another price. That other
price is the underlying; it can be a stock, an index, oil, an interest rate or
bitcoin. So you do not buy the thing itself, but an agreement about it.

Derivatives exist for two reasons. First, hedging: an airline wants to know
what jet fuel costs in six months. Second, speculation and arbitrage: anyone
with a view on the price can trade it with little capital, and anyone who finds
price differences between markets can collect them. For quants the second part
is the interesting one; but first you need to understand what a derivative
should cost.

## Futures: agree today, deliver later

A future is a standardised contract to buy or sell an underlying at a price
fixed today, on a later date. The buyer is long; the seller is short. Payment
happens at expiry.

- **Margin**: Instead of the full price you post collateral, often 5 to 20% of
  notional. That is where leverage comes from.
- **Mark-to-market**: Gains and losses are settled with your account daily (in
  crypto continuously). If margin drops below a threshold, you top up or the
  position is closed.
- **Settlement**: At expiry the underlying is either delivered (physical) or
  only the difference is paid (cash settlement). Crypto futures on CME and
  Deribit settle in cash.

### What a future should cost

Say bitcoin trades at 100 and you need it in one year. You can buy it today
and hold it; that ties up 100 of capital that would otherwise earn 4% interest.
Or you buy a future. For both routes to cost the same, the future has to trade
at about 104. In general:

<Formula>

$$
F = S \cdot e^{rT}
$$

- $$S$$: spot price
- $$r$$: interest rate p.a. (funding cost)
- $$T$$: time to expiry in years

</Formula>

This is not a model of the future but an arbitrage argument. If the future
trades clearly above this value, you buy spot, sell the future and deliver at
expiry. Your profit is fixed from day one, wherever the price ends up. This is
called **cash and carry**.

A future above spot is in **contango**; below spot it is in
**backwardation**. In crypto, contango is the norm, because many participants
want leveraged long exposure and pay a premium for it. The difference between
future and spot is the **basis**; annualised, it is the return you lock in
with cash and carry.

<BasisLab lang="en" />

Look at the table below the calculator: the total is the same in every row.
The price at expiry does not show up in the result; that is exactly what makes
the trade popular. It is still not risk free. The short side needs margin, and
if the price rises sharply you have to top it up, even though your spot gains
just as much.

## Options: a right, not an obligation

An option gives you the right to buy or sell an underlying at a fixed price
(the **strike**). Unlike a future you do not have to; you only exercise when
it pays. This right costs a **premium**, paid when you buy.

- **Call**: right to buy. Pays when the price is above the strike.
- **Put**: right to sell. Pays when the price is below the strike.
- **European vs. American**: European options can only be exercised at expiry,
  American ones at any time. Most crypto options (e.g. on Deribit) are
  European.
- **Moneyness**: A call with strike 90 at price 100 is _in the money_, with
  strike 110 _out of the money_, with strike 100 _at the money_.

The buyer can lose at most the premium. The seller (writer) collects the
premium for sure but carries the risk of a large move. Things get interesting
once you combine several options. In the lab below you can play through seven
standard strategies.

<PayoffLab lang="en" />

The solid line is your profit or loss at expiry, the dashed line the value
halfway through. The gap between them is the **time value**: before expiry an
option is worth more than its intrinsic value, because the price can still
move. Raise the volatility and watch the premium grow.

### What an option costs

In 1973 Fischer Black and Myron Scholes published a formula for the fair price
of a European option. The idea: an option can be rebuilt (replicated)
continuously from the underlying and cash. What the replication costs is the
fair price. For a call:

<Formula>

$$
\begin{aligned}
C &= S \, N(d_1) - K e^{-rT} N(d_2) \\[0.5em]
d_1 &= \frac{\ln(S/K) + \left(r + \frac{\sigma^2}{2}\right) T}{\sigma \sqrt{T}} \\[0.5em]
d_2 &= d_1 - \sigma \sqrt{T}
\end{aligned}
$$

- $$N$$: cumulative standard normal distribution
- $$\sigma$$: volatility p.a.

</Formula>

All inputs but one are observable: price, strike, time and rate. Volatility σ
is not. So in practice the formula is run backwards, and the market price gives
you the **implied volatility**. That is why option traders often talk in vol
instead of prices: "the 30-day call trades at 55 vol."

How sensitive the price is to each input is measured by the **Greeks**:

| Greek | Measures                               | Rule of thumb                                 |
| ----- | -------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------- |
| Delta | price change per 1 in the underlying   | ATM call ≈ 0.5; how much underlying you "own" |
| Gamma | change in delta per 1 in the underlying | largest ATM and close to expiry               |
| Vega  | price change per vol point             | long maturities react more                    |
| Theta | price change per day                   | buyers lose time value every day              |

One key relation holds without any model, **put-call parity**:
`C − P = S − K · e^(−rT)`. A long call plus a short put with the same strike is
the same as a future. If the market deviates, there is an arbitrage. The
calculator shows both sides of the equation; they always match.

<OptionPricer lang="en" />

The Monte Carlo part shows how to price options that have no closed formula:
simulate prices, average the payoffs, discount. With 1,000 paths you are often
still a few tenths off; with 100,000 paths the error is ten times smaller,
because it shrinks with 1/√N.

## Perpetuals: futures without expiry

A perpetual future (perp) is a future that never expires. The idea goes back
to a 1993 paper by Robert Shiller; it took off in 2016, when BitMEX launched the
first bitcoin perp. Today perps are the most traded product in crypto, with
clearly more volume than the spot market.

Without expiry, though, the mechanism that forces a normal future to converge
to spot is missing. The fix is **funding**: longs and shorts pay each other a
small amount at fixed intervals.

- If the perp trades **above** spot, funding is positive. Longs pay shorts.
  That makes being long more expensive and pushes the price towards spot.
- If the perp trades **below** spot, funding is negative. Shorts pay longs.

Most exchanges use the same rule:

<Formula>

$$
\text{funding} = P + \operatorname{clamp}_{\pm 0.05\%}\left(I - P\right)
$$

- $$P$$: premium index, perp versus spot, averaged
- $$I$$: interest component, usually 0.01% per 8 hours
- $$\operatorname{clamp}_{\pm a}$$: limited to the range −a to +a

</Formula>

As long as the premium index stays between −0.04% and +0.06%, funding is
exactly 0.01% per 8 hours. That sounds small; three times a day it adds up to
10.95% per year. For BTC, Binance and BitMEX pay every 8 hours, Hyperliquid
every hour (one eighth of the 8-hour rate each time). According to the
[BitMEX derivatives report for Q3 2025](https://blog.bitmex.com/2025q3-derivatives-report),
BTC funding on BitMEX, Binance and Hyperliquid was positive more than 92% of
the time. In the early years it sometimes exceeded 0.2% per 8 hours, which is
over 200% annualised.

### Leverage and liquidation

Perps can be traded with high leverage, 50× and more depending on the venue.
With leverage L you only post 1/L of notional. If the price moves roughly that
fraction against you (minus the maintenance margin), the exchange closes your
position. That is a **liquidation**.

<Formula>

$$
\begin{aligned}
\text{long:} \quad P_{\text{liq}} &\approx P_{\text{entry}} \left(1 - \tfrac{1}{L} + m\right) \\[0.5em]
\text{short:} \quad P_{\text{liq}} &\approx P_{\text{entry}} \left(1 + \tfrac{1}{L} - m\right)
\end{aligned}
$$

- $$L$$: leverage
- $$m$$: maintenance margin, e.g. 0.5%

</Formula>

If your margin does not cover a liquidation, the exchange's insurance fund
steps in. If that is empty too, profitable positions on the other side are
reduced automatically (**auto-deleveraging**, ADL). This hits exactly the
traders who are winning; for arbitrage strategies it is a real risk.

<LiquidationLab lang="en" />

The calculator converts the distance to liquidation into daily moves. At 60%
volatility the price moves about 3% on a normal day. With 20× leverage you are
less than two normal days away from liquidation.

## Crypto markets in five minutes

Crypto differs from traditional markets less in the maths than in the market
structure:

- **24/7**: No close, no holidays. Liquidity is thinner at night and on
  weekends, moves are often larger.
- **Fragmented**: The same coin trades at the same time on dozens of
  exchanges, against different stablecoins, and as spot, future, perp and
  option. Price differences are more common than for a stock with one home
  exchange.
- **CEX and DEX**: Centralised exchanges (Binance, Coinbase, Kraken, …) work
  like traditional exchanges with an order book and custody. Decentralised
  exchanges (Uniswap, Hyperliquid, …) run on a blockchain; you trade from your
  own wallet.
- **Stablecoins**: Tokens like USDT and USDC are meant to be worth $1 and are
  the actual trading currency. They can lose the peg, though; USDC briefly fell
  to about $0.88 in March 2023 after Silicon Valley Bank collapsed.
- **Counterparty risk**: On a CEX your balance is often legally just a claim
  against the exchange. At FTX, billions in customer funds were gone in
  November 2022.
- **Regulation**: In the EU the
  [MiCA regulation](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg/2023/1114/oj) has fully
  applied since the end of 2024; it licenses crypto service providers and
  stablecoin issuers.

### How a DEX sets prices

Many DEXs have no order book but an **automated market maker** (AMM). A pool
holds two tokens, e.g. ETH and USDC, and allows any swap as long as the product
of the reserves stays the same: `x · y = k`. The price follows from the ratio of
the reserves. Sell ETH into the pool and x rises, y falls, and ETH gets cheaper
in the pool. The larger your order relative to the pool, the worse your average
price.

<AmmLab lang="en" />

After your trade the pool quotes below the price on the large exchanges.
Arbitrageurs close this gap, often within the same block. The pool itself
"knows" nothing about the market price; arbitrage is the mechanism that keeps
it right. As the [Uniswap v2 whitepaper](https://uniswap.org/whitepaper.pdf)
describes, this is by design: trader fees pay liquidity providers for exactly
this risk.

## Where quants make money in crypto

The strategies split roughly into two groups. In **arbitrage-like trades** the
profit is almost fixed at entry; the risk lies in execution and in the
counterparty. In **statistical strategies** you have an edge on average but
lose individual trades. Arbitrage is rarely free: usually you pay with capital,
speed or a risk that does not show up in the model.

### Cash and carry with futures

You know this from the first calculator: buy spot, sell the quarterly future,
hold to expiry. The return is the annualised basis minus fees. Compared to the
funding carry it is fixed for the term; in return capital is tied up until
expiry.

### Funding carry with perps

The same idea with a perp: spot long, perp short, equal notional. Price moves
cancel out; what is left is the funding the short side receives. Because the
rate changes every few hours, the yield is not fixed. In return you can exit at
any time.

<FundingCarryLab lang="en" />

Try two things. First, set the holding period to a few days. The four fees
then eat a large part of the funding. Second, raise the leverage. The return on
your capital rises because you tie up less margin; at the same time the short
side's liquidation moves closer. That is the classic trade-off of this trade.

### Cross-exchange and triangular arbitrage

If bitcoin costs $100,000 on exchange A and $100,250 on exchange B, you buy on
A and sell on B. In practice nobody moves coins back and forth for this; it
takes too long. Instead you hold inventory on both exchanges and rebalance
occasionally.

In a **triangle** you trade three pairs on one exchange: USD → BTC → ETH → USD.
The ETH/BTC rate has to match the two dollar rates (ETH/USD divided by
BTC/USD). If it deviates and the deviation covers three fees, a profit is left.

<ArbitrageLab lang="en" />

These gaps are real, but small and short-lived. Whoever collects them reliably
has fast connections, low fee tiers and code that decides in milliseconds. For
getting started they are mainly a good exercise in clean fee and slippage
accounting.

### CEX-DEX arbitrage and MEV

The AMM calculator showed that every larger order pushes a pool away from the
market price. Arbitrageurs then buy cheaply in the pool and sell on a CEX (or
the other way round). On a blockchain there is an extra twist: the order of
transactions within a block is negotiable. The profit that can be extracted
from this ordering is called **MEV** (maximal extractable value). It ranges
from harmless arbitrage to front-running other users.

### Options arbitrage

Put-call parity has to hold on every exchange. With crypto options there are
also deviations between venues and between options and perps. A **box** (bull
call spread plus bear put spread) always pays the difference of the strikes at
expiry; its price reveals the implied interest rate. If that rate is clearly
higher than your own funding rate, you can sell the box and lock in the
difference.

### Market making

A market maker quotes buy and sell prices at the same time and earns the spread
between them. Sounds like safe money; the risk sits in two places.
**Inventory**: whoever just bought a lot loses if the price keeps falling.
**Adverse selection**: the people trading against you are often the ones who
know more than you. Good market making is therefore mostly good short-term
prediction plus risk management; exchanges often pay fee rebates for it.

### Trading volatility

An option's implied volatility is the market's forecast. Realised volatility is
what actually happens. If you sell an option and keep hedging its delta with
the underlying, you earn when realised vol stays below implied. On average
implied vol is higher (the **variance risk premium**); in return the strategy
loses a lot at once in crashes. Conversely, a delta-hedged buyer earns from
large moves (**gamma scalping**) and pays theta for it every day.

### Statistical arbitrage

Here there is no fixed relation, only a statistical one. One example is
**pairs trading**: BTC and ETH usually move alike. If their ratio widens
unusually far, you buy the cheaper coin, sell the more expensive one and wait
for the ratio to return. That works until the ratio shifts permanently. This is
why out-of-sample tests and clean backtests matter more here than the model
itself.

| Strategy             | You earn                        | Main risk                           |
| -------------------- | ------------------------------- | ----------------------------------- |
| Cash and carry       | basis until expiry              | margin, exchange failure            |
| Funding carry        | funding on the short side       | negative funding, liquidation, ADL  |
| Cross-exchange       | price difference                | latency, inventory on two venues    |
| Triangle             | mispriced cross rate            | execution, three fees               |
| CEX-DEX              | pool versus market gap          | gas, competition for the block      |
| Options arbitrage    | parity breaks, box rate         | liquidity, margin                   |
| Market making        | spread, fee rebates             | inventory, adverse selection        |
| Vol trading          | implied minus realised          | crashes, jumps                      |
| Statistical arb      | mean reversion                  | regime change, overfitting          |

## What goes wrong in practice

Almost all losses in "risk-free" trades come from things that are not in the
formula:

- **Counterparty**: An exchange halts withdrawals or goes bust. Both legs are
  then out of reach.
- **The hedge leg gets liquidated**: In a fast rally the short side loses
  before you can add margin. After that you are simply long.
- **Fees and slippage**: A few basis points decide. Always calculate with the
  price you actually get filled at, not the mid price.
- **Funding flips**: After a crash, funding is often negative for days.
- **Stablecoins**: Your "dollar" is a token with its own risk.
- **Overfitting**: A backtest with many parameters almost always finds
  something. Whether it is an edge only shows on a period the model has never
  seen.
